1. Same shape, not necessarily the same size

Similar figures have the same shape but need not have the same size. Congruent figures have both the same shape and the same size. Thus every congruent pair is similar, but a similar pair need not be congruent. The source examples are: all circles are similar, all squares are similar, and all equilateral triangles are similar. Two circles with different radii are not congruent, yet they are similar.

NCERT jemh106, Reprint 2026-27, printed pp73-74. Our explanation and translations; added examples labelled above.

2. Polygons need both conditions

Two polygons with the same number of sides are similar when their corresponding angles are equal and their corresponding sides are in the same ratio. Do not decide by appearance alone. Fix the vertex correspondence first, then compare each corresponding angle and side pair using that same matching. Stretching a figure more in one direction is not a uniform enlargement.

NCERT jemh106, Reprint 2026-27, printed pp75-77. Our explanation and translations; added examples labelled above.

3. Scale factor: keep the ratio direction

The source describes enlarging a photograph from 35 mm to 45 mm. Corresponding length in the larger photo / length in the smaller photo = 45/35 = 9/7. The reverse ratio 35/45 = 7/9 is the reduction factor. Every corresponding length must use the same factor, while corresponding angles stay equal. Our own example: enlarging a 2 by 3 rectangle to 4 by 6 gives 4/2 = 6/3 = 2. Lengths double and angles stay the same, so the rectangles are similar.

NCERT jemh106, Reprint 2026-27, printed pp75-76. Our explanation and translations; added examples labelled above.

4. Why one condition alone is not enough

In the source Fig.6.6, a square and a rectangle have equal corresponding angles but their sides are not proportional. In Fig.6.7, a square and a non-square rhombus have proportional sides but unequal corresponding angles. Neither pair is similar (figures are not reproduced here). Our own numerical check: a 2 by 3 rectangle and a 4 by 4 square both have 90° angles, but the ratios 4/2 = 2 and 4/3 differ. They are not similar. This is the general polygon test; triangle-specific criteria come later.

NCERT jemh106, Reprint 2026-27, printed p77. Our explanation and translations; added examples labelled above.

6.1: समरूप क्या होता है और यह क्यों काम आता है

नया शब्द: सर्वांगसम = वही आकार और वही माप। समरूप = वही आकार, माप अलग हो सकता है। पुस्तक कहती है इस अध्याय में समरूप आकृतियाँ, खासकर त्रिभुज, पढ़ेंगे और पाइथागोरस प्रमेय का सरल प्रमाण देंगे।

सर्वांगसम आकृतियाँ समरूप होती हैं, पर समरूप आकृतियाँ सर्वांगसम होना ज़रूरी नहीं। सभी वृत्त समरूप; सभी वर्ग समरूप; सभी समबाहु त्रिभुज समरूप। वृत्त और वर्ग समरूप नहीं (आकार अलग)। अप्रत्यक्ष माप: एवरेस्ट की ऊँचाई या चाँद की दूरी फीते से नहीं नापते; यह समरूपता के सिद्धांत पर आधारित है (छपा: उदाहरण 7, प्रश्नावली 6.3 प्र.15, और अध्याय 8-9)।

6.2: फ़ोटो से समरूपता की परिभाषा

पुस्तक फ़ोटो का उदाहरण लेती है। ताजमहल की तीन अलग माप की फ़ोटो समरूप हैं। एक ही माप की दो फ़ोटो (10 वर्ष और 40 वर्ष की आयु की) समरूप नहीं, क्योंकि आकार अलग है।

फ़ोटोग्राफ़र छोटी फ़िल्म (35 mm) को 45 mm या 55 mm तक बड़ा करता है। हर रेखाखंड 35:45 (या 35:55) के अनुपात में बढ़ता है, या उलटे 45:35 (55:35) में घटता है। संगत कोण हमेशा बराबर रहते हैं। परिभाषा (छपी): समान भुजाओं की संख्या वाले दो बहुभुज समरूप हैं यदि (i) संगत कोण बराबर हों और (ii) संगत भुजाएँ एक ही अनुपात (समानुपात) में हों। इस समान अनुपात को स्केल फ़ैक्टर (Representative Fraction) कहते हैं; नक्शे और ब्लूप्रिंट इसी से बनते हैं।

6.1: what similar means and why it is useful

New term: congruent = same shape and same size. Similar = same shape, size may differ. The book says this chapter studies similar figures, mainly triangles, and gives a simple proof of Pythagoras Theorem.

Congruent figures are similar, but similar figures need not be congruent. All circles are similar; all squares are similar; all equilateral triangles are similar. A circle and a square are not similar (different shape). Indirect measurement: the height of Everest or the distance to the moon is not measured with a tape; it rests on the principle of similarity (printed: Example 7, Exercise 6.3 Q.15, and Chapters 8-9).

Our check: two circles of radius 2 and 5 are not congruent (sizes differ) but are similar (same shape). Two circles of radius 3 and 3 are both congruent and similar.

NCERT jemh106, Reprint 2026-27, printed pp73-76, Sections 6.1-6.2 (Figs. 6.1-6.3 not reproduced). The ratio arithmetic and glosses are ours.

6.2: the photograph idea behind the definition

The book uses photographs. Three Taj Mahal photographs of different sizes are similar. Two same-size photographs of one person at age 10 and age 40 are not similar, because the shape differs.

A photographer enlarges a 35 mm film to 45 mm or 55 mm. Every line segment grows in the ratio 35:45 (or 35:55), or equally shrinks in the ratio 45:35 (55:35). Corresponding angles stay equal. Definition (printed): two polygons with the same number of sides are similar if (i) corresponding angles are equal and (ii) corresponding sides are in the same ratio (proportion). The common ratio is the scale factor (Representative Fraction); maps and blueprints use it.

Our check: 45/35 = 9/7 ≈ 1.29 and 55/35 = 11/7 ≈ 1.57, the same ratio on every segment of one photo. Equal angles alone, or proportional sides alone, are not enough: the book's Fig. 6.6 (square-rectangle) and Fig. 6.7 (square-rhombus) show this.

1. Triangles ke liye similarity ki do conditions

Triangle bhi ek polygon hai, isliye do triangles tab similar hain jab (i) unke corresponding angles equal hon aur (ii) unki corresponding sides same ratio (proportion) mein hon. Jin do triangles ke corresponding angles equal hon, unhe equiangular triangles kehte hain.

3. Theorem 6.1: Basic Proportionality Theorem (Thales)

Statement: If a line is drawn parallel to one side of a triangle to intersect the other two sides in distinct points, the other two sides are divided in the same ratio.

Printed proof (Fig.6.10 shabdon mein: triangle ABC mein DE ∥ BC, D side AB par, E side AC par; BE aur CD joined, DM perpendicular AC, EN perpendicular AB). Diya gaya: triangle ABC, DE ∥ BC. Prove karna hai: AD/DB = AE/EC. Proof ke steps area ke through:

ar(ADE) = 1/2 x AD x EN (EN, AB par perpendicular height) ar(BDE) = 1/2 x DB x EN isliye ar(ADE)/ar(BDE) = AD/DB ... (1) ar(ADE) = 1/2 x AE x DM (DM, AC par perpendicular height) ar(DEC) = 1/2 x EC x DM isliye ar(ADE)/ar(DEC) = AE/EC ... (2) Triangles BDE aur DEC same base DE par hain aur same parallels BC aur DE ke beech hain, isliye ar(BDE) = ar(DEC) ... (3) (1), (2) aur (3) se: AD/DB = AE/EC

Yaad rakhne wala point: proof ka engine areas ka ratio hai - same height wale triangles ke areas unke bases ke ratio mein hote hain, aur BDE/DEC same base + same parallels wali trick se dono ratios jud jaate hain.

1. The two similarity conditions for triangles

A triangle is also a polygon, so two triangles are similar when (i) their corresponding angles are equal and (ii) their corresponding sides are in the same ratio (proportion). Two triangles whose corresponding angles are equal are called equiangular triangles.

The Greek mathematician Thales (640-546 B.C.) gave an important result: the ratio of any two corresponding sides in two equiangular triangles is always the same. He is believed to have used the Basic Proportionality Theorem (now called the Thales Theorem) for this. That theorem is the next note.

NCERT jemh106, Reprint 2026-27, printed p79. Our explanation and translations.

2. Activity 2: a parallel line gives equal ratios

3. Theorem 6.1: Basic Proportionality Theorem (Thales)

Statement: If a line is drawn parallel to one side of a triangle to intersect the other two sides in distinct points, the other two sides are divided in the same ratio.

Printed proof (Fig.6.10 in words: in triangle ABC, DE ∥ BC with D on AB and E on AC; BE and CD joined, DM perpendicular to AC, EN perpendicular to AB). Given: triangle ABC with DE ∥ BC. To prove: AD/DB = AE/EC. The proof runs through areas:

ar(ADE) = 1/2 x AD x EN (EN is the height on AB) ar(BDE) = 1/2 x DB x EN so ar(ADE)/ar(BDE) = AD/DB ... (1) ar(ADE) = 1/2 x AE x DM (DM is the height on AC) ar(DEC) = 1/2 x EC x DM so ar(ADE)/ar(DEC) = AE/EC ... (2) Triangles BDE and DEC are on the same base DE and between the same parallels BC and DE, so ar(BDE) = ar(DEC) ... (3) From (1), (2) and (3): AD/DB = AE/EC

Point to remember: the engine of the proof is area ratios - triangles with the same height have areas in the ratio of their bases, and the same-base-same-parallels trick for BDE and DEC ties the two ratios together.

4. Activity 3: same ratio means a parallel line

Now the reverse question (converse): if a line divides two sides of a triangle in the same ratio, must it be parallel to the third side? Construction (Fig.6.11 in words, not reproduced): draw any angle XAY. On ray AX mark points B1, B2, B3, B4, B so that AB1 = B1B2 = B2B3 = B3B4 = B4B. In the same way mark C1, C2, C3, C4, C on ray AY. Join B1C1 and BC.

AB1/B1B = AC1/C1C (= 1/4) and B1C1 ∥ BC AB2/B2B = AC2/C2C (= 2/3) and B2C2 ∥ BC AB3/B3B = AC3/C3C (= 3/2) and B3C3 ∥ BC AB4/B4B = AC4/C4C (= 4/1) and B4C4 ∥ BC

Observation: whenever the two ratios match, the joined line comes out parallel to BC. Repeating with any angle and any number of equal parts gives the same result. This is what gives the converse of Theorem 6.1.

5. Theorem 6.2: converse of the BPT

Statement: If a line divides any two sides of a triangle in the same ratio, then the line is parallel to the third side.

Printed proof (Fig.6.12 in words: in triangle ABC, AD/DB = AE/EC; assume DE is not parallel to BC; then draw DE′ ∥ BC from D, meeting AC at E′). The reason for each printed "Why?" is ours, in square brackets:

AD/DB = AE′/E′C [Theorem 6.1, since DE′ ∥ BC] so AE/EC = AE′/E′C [both equal AD/DB] add 1 to both sides: AE/EC + 1 = AE′/E′C + 1 (AE+EC)/EC = (AE′+E′C)/E′C AC/EC = AC/E′C so EC = E′C, which means E and E′ are the same point [equal lengths on the same segment AC] so DE ∥ BC

The method is contradiction: first assume the line is not parallel, then show that this assumption is impossible.

6. Example 1: using the theorem (AD/AB = AE/AC)

Printed question: a line intersects sides AB and AC of triangle ABC at D and E respectively and is parallel to BC; prove that AD/AB = AE/AC. (Fig.6.13 in words: a standard triangle with DE ∥ BC.)

DE ∥ BC (Given) AD/DB = AE/EC (Theorem 6.1) inverting: DB/AD = EC/AE add 1 to both sides: DB/AD + 1 = EC/AE + 1 (DB+AD)/AD = (EC+AE)/AE AB/AD = AC/AE so AD/AB = AE/AC

8. Example 3: proving a triangle isosceles

Printed question (Fig.6.16): in triangle PQR, S is on side PQ and T is on side PR. Given PS/SQ = PT/TR and angle PST = angle PRQ. Prove that PQR is an isosceles triangle.

1. The symbol ~ and correct correspondence

When triangles ABC and DEF are similar (corresponding angles equal, corresponding sides in the same ratio) we write △ABC ~ △DEF - read "triangle ABC is similar to triangle DEF". The symbol ~ stands for "is similar to". In Class IX you used ≅ for congruence.

3. Theorem 6.3: the AAA similarity criterion

Statement: If in two triangles, corresponding angles are equal, then their corresponding sides are in the same ratio (or proportion) and hence the two triangles are similar. This is the AAA (Angle-Angle-Angle) criterion.

6. Theorem 6.4: the SSS similarity criterion

In △ABE and △CFB: ∠A = ∠C (opposite angles of a parallelogram) AD ∥ BC (parallelogram) and E is on AD produced ⇒ AE ∥ BC BE is a transversal ⇒ ∠AEB = ∠CBF (alternate angles) ⇒ △ABE ~ △CFB (AA similarity criterion)

Watch the correspondence: A↔C, E↔B, B↔F - that is why the symbolic form is △ABE ~ △CFB.

Q9: in Fig 6.39, △ABC ~ △AMP and CA/PA = BC/MP

Q10: angle bisectors CD and GH - a ratio and two more pairs

Printed question: CD and GH are bisectors of ∠ACB and ∠FGE respectively, D on side AB, H on side FE, and △ABC ~ △FEG. Show: (i) CD/GH = AC/FG, (ii) △DCB ~ △HGE, (iii) △DCA ~ △HGF. (This question has no printed figure.)

△ABC ~ △FEG (given) ⇒ ∠A = ∠F, ∠B = ∠E, ∠ACB = ∠FGE CD bisects ∠ACB ⇒ ∠ACD = ∠DCB = (1/2)∠ACB GH bisects ∠FGE ⇒ ∠FGH = ∠HGE = (1/2)∠FGE ⇒ ∠ACD = ∠DCB = ∠FGH = ∠HGE (i) In △ACD and △FGH: ∠A = ∠F, ∠ACD = ∠FGH ⇒ △ACD ~ △FGH (AA) ⇒ CD/GH = AC/FG (corresponding sides) (ii) In △DCB and △HGE: ∠B = ∠E, ∠DCB = ∠HGE ⇒ △DCB ~ △HGE (AA) (iii) In △DCA and △HGF: ∠A = ∠F, ∠DCA = ∠HGF ⇒ △DCA ~ △HGF (AA)

Part (i) says: corresponding angle bisectors of similar triangles stand in the same ratio as the similarity ratio itself.

Q11: isosceles ABC, E on CB produced - △ABD ~ △ECF

Printed question: in Fig.6.40, E is a point on side CB produced of an isosceles triangle ABC with AB = AC. AD ⊥ BC and EF ⊥ AC. Prove that △ABD ~ △ECF. (Figure in words: A top; on the baseline, left to right E, B, D, C; AD ⊥ BC at D; F on side AC with EF ⊥ AC at F.)

AB = AC (given) ⇒ ∠ABC = ∠ACB (angles opposite equal sides) ∠ABD = ∠ABC (D lies on BC) and ∠ECF = ∠ACB (same angle at C) ⇒ ∠ABD = ∠ECF In △ABD and △ECF: ∠ABD = ∠ECF (from above) ∠ADB = ∠EFC = 90° (AD ⊥ BC, EF ⊥ AC) ⇒ △ABD ~ △ECF (AA similarity criterion)

Q12: two sides + median proportional - △ABC ~ △PQR

Printed question: sides AB and BC and median AD of a triangle ABC are respectively proportional to sides PQ and QR and median PM of △PQR (see Fig.6.41). Show that △ABC ~ △PQR. (Figure in words: two triangles - △ABC with A top, B/C bottom, D the midpoint of BC; △PQR with P top, Q/R bottom, M the midpoint of QR.)

D and M are midpoints ⇒ BC = 2BD, QR = 2QM Given: AB/PQ = BC/QR = AD/PM ⇒ AB/PQ = BD/QM = AD/PM (since BC/QR = 2BD/2QM = BD/QM) In △ABD and △PQM all three sides are proportional ⇒ △ABD ~ △PQM (SSS similarity criterion) ⇒ ∠ABD = ∠PQM, that is ∠ABC = ∠PQR Now in △ABC and △PQR: AB/PQ = BC/QR (given) ∠ABC = ∠PQR (from above, included angle) ⇒ △ABC ~ △PQR (SAS similarity criterion)

Q13: ∠ADC = ∠BAC gives CA² = CB.CD

Printed question: D is a point on side BC of a triangle ABC such that ∠ADC = ∠BAC. Show that CA² = CB.CD. (This question has no printed figure.)

In △ABC and △DAC: ∠BAC = ∠ADC (given) ∠ACB = ∠DCA (common angle C) ⇒ △ABC ~ △DAC (AA similarity criterion) ⇒ CA/CD = CB/CA (corresponding sides) Cross-multiply ⇒ CA² = CB.CD

Watch the correspondence: A↔D, B↔A, C↔C - that is why we write △ABC ~ △DAC. CA is a side of both triangles, which is what produces the square.

Q14: AB, AC + median proportional - △ABC ~ △PQR (with construction)

Printed question: sides AB and AC and median AD of a triangle ABC are respectively proportional to sides PQ and PR and median PM of another triangle PQR. Show that △ABC ~ △PQR. (This question has no printed figure.)

Construction (ours, for the proof): extend AD to E so that AD = DE, and extend PM to N so that PM = MN. Join BE and QN. D is the midpoint of both BC and AE ⇒ ABEC is a parallelogram ⇒ BE = CA Likewise M is the midpoint of both QR and PN ⇒ PQNR is a parallelogram ⇒ QN = PR AE = 2AD and PN = 2PM ⇒ AE/PN = AD/PM In △ABE and △PQN: AB/PQ = BE/QN = AE/PN (given + steps above) ⇒ △ABE ~ △PQN (SSS similarity criterion) ⇒ ∠ABE = ∠PQN In the parallelogram BE ∥ CA ⇒ ∠ABE + ∠BAC = 180°; likewise ∠PQN + ∠QPR = 180° ⇒ ∠BAC = ∠QPR Now in △ABC and △PQR: AB/PQ = AC/PR (given) ∠BAC = ∠QPR (included angle, from above) ⇒ △ABC ~ △PQR (SAS similarity criterion)

Q15: shadows of a pole and a tower - height of the tower

Printed question: a vertical pole of length 6 m casts a shadow 4 m long on the ground and at the same time a tower casts a shadow 28 m long. Find the height of the tower. (This question has no printed figure.)

Same time ⇒ the sun's rays make the same angle for both Pole and tower are both vertical ⇒ each makes 90° with the ground ⇒ the two right triangles are similar (AA: right angle + sun's common angle) ⇒ height/shadow ratio is the same: tower height / 28 = 6/4 Tower height = 28 × 6/4 = 42 m

Q16: the ratio of medians of similar triangles

Printed question: AD and PM are medians of triangles ABC and PQR respectively, where △ABC ~ △PQR. Prove that AB/PQ = AD/PM. (This question has no printed figure.)

△ABC ~ △PQR (given) ⇒ AB/PQ = BC/QR and ∠ABC = ∠PQR (similar triangles) D, M are midpoints ⇒ BC = 2BD, QR = 2QM ⇒ BC/QR = BD/QM ⇒ AB/PQ = BD/QM In △ABD and △PQM: AB/PQ = BD/QM (from above) ∠ABD = ∠PQM (∠B = ∠Q, included angle) ⇒ △ABD ~ △PQM (SAS similarity criterion) ⇒ AB/PQ = AD/PM (corresponding sides)

Summary points 1-3: similar figures, congruence, polygon test

The book's printed summary points, kept close to the printed wording:

1. Two figures having the same shape but not necessarily the same size are called similar figures. 2. All the congruent figures are similar but the converse is not true. 3. Two polygons of the same number of sides are similar, if (i) their corresponding angles are equal and (ii) their corresponding sides are in the same ratio (i.e., proportion).

Point 2 says: congruence needs the same shape and the same size; similarity needs only the shape. So every congruent pair is similar, but not every similar pair is congruent.

Summary points 4-5: BPT and its converse

4. If a line is drawn parallel to one side of a triangle to intersect the other two sides in distinct points, then the other two sides are divided in the same ratio. 5. If a line divides any two sides of a triangle in the same ratio, then the line is parallel to the third side.

Point 4 is the Basic Proportionality Theorem (Thales, 640-546 B.C.), point 5 is its converse - both printed proofs are covered on this page in Section 6.3.

Summary points 6-9: the four similarity criteria + RHS note

6. If in two triangles, corresponding angles are equal, then their corresponding sides are in the same ratio and hence the two triangles are similar (AAA similarity criterion). 7. If in two triangles, two angles of one triangle are respectively equal to the two angles of the other triangle, then the two triangles are similar (AA similarity criterion). 8. If in two triangles, corresponding sides are in the same ratio, then their corresponding angles are equal and hence the triangles are similar (SSS similarity criterion). 9. If one angle of a triangle is equal to one angle of another triangle and the sides including these angles are in the same ratio (proportional), then the triangles are similar (SAS similarity criterion).

A NOTE TO THE READER (printed): if in two right triangles, hypotenuse and one side of one triangle are proportional to the hypotenuse and one side of the other triangle, then the two triangles are similar. This may be referred to as the RHS Similarity Criterion. The book adds: using it in Example 2, Chapter 8 makes the proof simpler.
